A plan, not a build

The answer book

A helper that learns what Fayr already knows how to say, keeps it written down in plain English where anyone can check it, and eventually offers it back — first to the person answering a user, later to the user directly. No code yet. This is the shape of the thing and an honest account of what we have to start from.

Why it is worth building

People write to Fayr about a small number of things. The app's own help screen already offers four subjects to pick from, and they are the four: my refund has not arrived, my order was not found, my review is not being detected, a problem with my withdrawal.

The good answers to those exist. Some are already written into the app — the refund policy, the three questions on every offer page. The rest live in whoever answered the last one, and in threads that get closed and never read again. That is the whole opportunity: not new knowledge, but knowledge that is already being produced and then dropped.

Four stages, in this order

The order matters more than the pieces. Each stage is useful on its own, and each one earns the right to the next by being checked by a person first.

Stage one

Write the answers down, in one place, in plain words

A collection of short entries: a question as a person would ask it, and the answer as Fayr would give it. Nothing clever — a list somebody can read end to end in ten minutes and say "that one is wrong".

It starts seeded from what is already written: the refund policy, the per-offer questions, the four subjects, the terms on each campaign. No model is involved at this stage at all.

Useful immediately, even if the project stopped here: new support staff have something to read, and the answers stop depending on who is on shift.
Stage two

Learn from yesterday, once a night

A job that runs each night, reads the threads answered that day, and proposes changes to the book: a new entry for a question nobody had written down, or a better wording for one that already existed.

It proposes. It does not write. Every suggestion waits for a support person to accept, edit or throw away, and the book records who accepted what and when — so a wrong answer can always be traced to a decision rather than to a machine.

The book grows by itself, and stays something a person signed off. This is the same arrangement the screenshot checks already use.
Stage three

Offer the answer to staff, while they are typing

A support person opens a question. Beside the reply box, the two or three entries from the book that look relevant, and a draft reply built from them. They read it, change what they want, and send it — or ignore it entirely.

The draft is never sent on its own. Whether staff used it is recorded, because that is the only honest measure of whether any of this is working: if they keep ignoring it, the book is wrong, not the staff.

Answers get faster and more consistent, and nothing reaches a user that a person did not send.
Stage four

Answer the user in the app — within hard limits

Before someone writes to Fayr at all, show them what the book says about the thing they are asking. Most of the four common questions have a true, general answer that needs no human.

But two lines cannot be crossed. It must never answer a question about this person's own money — where their refund is, why their amount is what it is, when their withdrawal will land. And it must never state a rule that is not in the book, because the book is the part a person has checked. Anything outside those, it hands to a human, in one tap, without making the user ask twice.

Fewer questions reach the queue, and the ones that do are the ones that genuinely need a person.

What already exists to build on

All of this is in the product today. None of it needs designing again.

  • Real questions and real answers, already accumulating. Every question and every staff reply is stored, timestamped, and linked to the staff member who wrote it. That is the raw material, and it is already being collected.
  • A queue where staff already answer. The Questions tab in the staff console, with the reply box that stage three would sit beside. No new place for anyone to go.
  • Four categories of problem, drawn from real use. The subjects on the app's help screen are a working taxonomy of what people write about.
  • Answers already written in plain English. The refund policy's nine sections, the three questions on every offer page, and the terms on each campaign. Stage one is largely a matter of collecting these.
  • A model connection, already wired and already used this way. The Anthropic library is a dependency, the client is built only when a key is present, and the screenshot checks already call a model to read something and then hand the result to a person. The pattern is proven here, not borrowed.
  • The propose-then-approve arrangement itself. The screenshot verification flow is exactly this shape: a model extracts, a support person approves, nothing moves on its own, every step audited. Stages two and three are the same arrangement applied to words.
  • A nightly job with somewhere to live. Two scheduled jobs already run, one at a time across instances, with a lock that stops two servers doing the same work.
  • A trail of who did what. Staff actions are already audited, so "who approved this answer" is a question the system is already built to answer.
  • Care with personal detail, already established. Verification screenshots are private data, stored outside the public space and reachable only through a permission-checked endpoint. The same standard applies to support threads, and the precedent exists.

What has to be built from nothing

  • The book itself. There is no store of answers today, in any form. An entry needs a question, an answer, a category, and a state — draft, live, retired.
  • Review and history for the book. Who approved an entry, when, and what it replaced. Without this, "a person checked it" is a claim rather than a record.
  • The nightly reading job. Reads yesterday's answered threads and proposes entries. The scheduling exists; the job does not.
  • Stripping personal detail. Threads are full of names, order numbers and amounts. The book must carry none of it, and that has to be enforced rather than trusted — an answer that quotes one user's order number to another is the worst thing this project could do.
  • Finding the right entry for a live question. Matching what somebody just wrote to the two or three entries that actually help.
  • The suggestion panel beside the reply box, and the record of whether staff used what it offered.
  • The in-app answer surface, and the enforced limits on it — no answering about a person's own money, nothing outside the book.
  • A way to tell whether it is any good. How often staff accept the draft, how often users still write in after reading an answer, how many entries were corrected. Without these the project cannot be judged, only believed.

Three things that are true and awkward

There is almost nothing to learn from yet

The database holds two questions and one staff reply. A helper that learns from real support traffic has, today, essentially no traffic to learn from. That is why stage one seeds from what is already written rather than waiting for volume — and it is why stage two's value arrives slowly, one real thread at a time.

Every thread contains personal detail

People writing about a stuck refund name their order, their amount, and often themselves. Stripping that out is not a tidy-up step at the end; it decides whether stage two can run at all.

The most-asked question is the one it must not answer

"Where is my refund?" is both the commonest question and the one that depends entirely on one person's own task, amount and hold. The book can explain how refunds work. It cannot tell somebody where theirs is, and pretending otherwise would turn the best answer in the book into the most damaging one.

What I would do first

Stage one, by hand, with no model anywhere near it: collect the answers that already exist in the app into one reviewable list, and let support correct it. That alone is worth having, it costs almost nothing, and it is the thing every later stage stands on. If the list turns out to be wrong or nobody reads it, we have learned that for the price of an afternoon rather than a quarter.
